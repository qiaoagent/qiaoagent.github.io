# Why I left medicine for AI

Wei Zexi was a computer science student in Xi'an. In 2014, in his second year, he was diagnosed with advanced synovial sarcoma, a rare cancer. When chemotherapy and radiotherapy failed, his family searched Baidu for other options. The top result was a paid advertisement for a hospital in Beijing that offered a "biological immunotherapy", claiming an 80 to 90% success rate and a collaboration with Stanford. Neither claim was true: the therapy was experimental, its trials in the United States had stopped, and Stanford had no such partnership. The family spent about $30,000 (more than 200,000 yuan) on it, and it did nothing. Wei died in April 2016 at 21. I was a second-year medical student then, two years younger than him, and his story convinced me that accurate medical information can matter as much as the right treatment, and that people deserve a much better medical search engine. That is what I set out to build.

At the time I was set on becoming a surgeon, and if nothing had intervened I would be standing at an operating table today. Instead, what sits in front of me is a few dark screens running green code. Many idealistic people left Baidu for medicine after the case, and other companies entered medical search. Unfortunately, most people in China who fall ill today still start with Baidu.

I was one of the people in that wave who wanted to build medical search. Two years as a visiting scholar in the United States coincided with the golden age of NLP, taught me the technical side, and settled my decision to leave medicine for AI. The three years back in China were split: clinical rotations at Peking Union Medical College Hospital to finish the MD, and the lab and Alibaba DAMO Academy to keep my hands on the methods. When it was time to find a job, I interviewed at one place, NIH, where I work now. One duty of our lab is maintaining the search algorithm of PubMed, the most used medical search engine in the world, with millions of searches every day.

Three months after I joined NIH, ChatGPT arrived. Search dropped from the last step of getting information to an intermediate step: after searching, AI can now help people understand what they found. The way people get medical information is being fundamentally changed, and I hope our work can accelerate that change and make the world a little better.

Looking back, I do not see giving up the white coat as an escape. It was a choice to face a broader field. From Wei Zexi to today, every advance in AI and every improvement to a medical information platform has confirmed that the direction is worth it. I no longer wear the white coat, but in my mind I am still the intern doing his best to answer a patient's question.
